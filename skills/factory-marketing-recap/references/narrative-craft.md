# Narrative craft — how the story gets told

Applies to `transcript.md`, `talk-track.md`, and the choice of what goes on the board at all.
Run `factory-humanizer` on the actual sentences; this file governs the argument, not the prose.

Illustrated below with one worked example (a market-commentary video reacting to a currency
intervention) — the techniques generalize to any recurring subject a recap covers, not just
financial markets. The source is credited because knowing where a technique came from is how you
check whether it still fits your subject.

---

## 0. The spine

A week (or whatever period this recap covers) has many things in it. A viewer will remember one.

**Every recap picks a spine before anything else gets written:** the single story the whole
package is built to land. Everything else on the board exists to feed it, contradict it, or
date it. The spine is chosen in Phase 1 (research), written down at the top of the sources file,
and does not change once the board starts.

The spine is what earns the deep treatment below. The remaining clusters stay as they are:
a number, a comparison, a caption. Do not try to give every cluster a mechanism and a metaphor.
That is how you get an illustrated report instead of a recap.

> **Format is an open, project-level question — do not silently decide it yourself.** One deep
> story for the whole runtime, or a roundup with one story developed and the rest as short items,
> are both legitimate shapes. Confirm which this project wants before assuming.

---

## 1. The beat sheet

The order to reach for when the spine has any depth to it. Skip beats that don't apply. Do not
reorder 2 and 3.

| # | Beat | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Physical hook** | A thing you can hold, and one human-scale number attached to it |
| 2 | **What it means for you** | Consequences before mechanism. Two audiences, if relevant — the people this affects one way, and the people it affects the other way |
| 3 | **The puzzle, stated out loud** | Name the thing that does not add up, and say you are going to answer it |
| 4 | **Reject the official explanation** | Quote what the authorities/official source said, then refuse it. This is the engine |
| 5 | **History, as long as it needs** | How the present became structurally inevitable |
| 6 | **The mechanism** | The actual machine, explained so a non-expert follows it |
| 7 | **The metaphor** | One image that holds the mechanism, reused to the end |
| 8 | **The selfish stake** | Why a viewer with no direct exposure to this subject should care |
| 9 | **Did it work?** | The verdict, flatly |
| 10 | **The detail that lands it** | One fact, often about a person, that converts the verdict into a claim |
| 11 | **Why it will not be fixed** | Or what would have to change |
| 12 | **Your own thesis** | Not a summary of what was said |

## 2. Open on something you can hold

The worked example opened on a banknote and a meal you could buy with it, then asked what that was
in the viewer's own currency — two minutes before any chart.

A board that opens on an index level, a raw metric, or a percentage is a number nobody has
intuition for. A price someone actually pays, converted into terms the viewer understands
instantly, means something.

**Rule:** the spine gets one physical, human-scale anchor before its first raw number or
percentage. A price someone actually pays. A salary. A menu. A ticket. Convert it into terms the
viewer understands and say the old number and the new one.

## 3. Consequences before mechanism, for every audience this affects differently

The worked example spent a minute on what the event does to one group, then a minute on what it
does to a different group with the opposite exposure — only then did it explain the mechanism.

**Rule:** before the mechanism, say who is better off and who is worse off. Name both, if the
subject has two sides. The same fact is often good news to one audience and bad news to another.

## 4. State the puzzle, then refuse the official answer

The worked example named the anomaly plainly, played the official explanation, and rejected it.
That refusal is what powers everything after it, because the viewer now wants the real reason.

**Rule:** find the period's stated-reason-that-does-not-hold. Put the official line on the board
in the source's own words, then put the reason it fails next to it. A recap with no rejected
explanation has no engine and reads as a list.

## 5. History earns its length

The worked example spent roughly a quarter of its runtime on decades of background. It was not
background — it was the proof that the present is structural rather than a one-off.

**Rule:** if the spine is structural, draw the long series and go back as far as the cause goes.
A default window (e.g. one month) is the wrong picture when the real story is decades old.

## 6. Explain the mechanism as dialogue

The worked example turned a piece of market mechanics into a two-sided conversation between a
borrower and a lender.

**Rule:** when a mechanism has two sides, write it as two speakers. It removes jargon
automatically, because people do not say technical shorthand out loud in conversation. This pairs
with the plain-language rule in the talk-track phase and does the same job more cheaply.

## 7. One metaphor, carried to the end

The best thing in the worked example was a single physical metaphor for the whole mechanism,
reused and extended for the entire runtime — every later reference to the actors in the story used
the metaphor's own vocabulary, right through the closing image.

**Rule for the metaphor:**

- One per recap, for the spine only. A board with three metaphors has none.
- It must be **mechanically load-bearing**: every part maps to a real part of the mechanism, and
  the mapping survives being pushed on. A metaphor that only works for the headline is decoration
  and gets cut.
- It must have a **prediction inside it**. A good metaphor is still doing work in the closing
  minute because it forecasts what happens next.
- **Draw it on the board.** This is the one cluster that should be an actual drawing rather than a
  chart or a box of text.
- **Reuse the vocabulary** in every later cluster and in the talk track. A metaphor used once and
  dropped should be deleted from the board and the space reclaimed.

## 8. Make the stake selfish

The strongest turn in the worked example connected a story with no obvious relevance to the
audience's own finances, through one clear causal channel.

**Rule:** the spine has to answer "why should someone with no direct exposure watch this." Find
the channel by which it reaches them. Add this to Phase 1's research as a question, not an
afterthought.

## 9. Comedy is load-bearing

The worked example used a joke to *explain* a genuinely confusing mechanism, not to decorate a
slide.

**Rule:** jokes are allowed when the joke *is* the explanation. A joke that could be deleted
without losing information gets deleted. The test is strict: does removing it cost the viewer a
fact?

## 10. Leave the mess visible

The worked example's presenter noticed on camera that he'd used the wrong version of a chart, said
so, and moved on — a polished deck would have cut that. It's the clearest signal a viewer is
watching someone think rather than present.

**Rule:** do not sand the board to a finish. A crossed-out number, a note in the margin, a box
that is visibly a later addition are all correct. Draw your own visuals rather than screenshotting
someone else's branded chart — same instinct: it should look genuinely drawn, not assembled.

## 11. One detail, usually a person, lands the argument

The worked example found a single biographical fact about an actor in the story that made their
own behavior testify against their stated position — far stronger, far cheaper than paragraphs of
analysis.

**Rule:** for the spine, look for the biographical or institutional fact that makes an actor's own
behavior testify about what they actually believe. Add it to Phase 1: *who did this, and what does
their history say about whether they believed it would work?*

## 12. End on the thesis, not the summary

The worked example closed on a position, then handed off — no recap of the recap.

Ending on an open question is the floor, not the ceiling. The strongest close is a position the
presenter is willing to be wrong about in public.

---

## 13. Explain the engine, not just the event

The single most important rule in this file. A board can show an event happening and its outcome
without ever explaining **why it happened** — leaving a fact with no cause behind it, and no way
for a viewer to judge whether it's likely to continue.

**Rule:** for the spine, the board must carry the causal chain that produces the fact, not only
the fact. If you cannot draw the chain, the research is not finished. Test: could a viewer predict
what happens next from what's on the board? If not, the mechanism is missing.

---

## 14. Where the recap has an edge over any single source

Worth remembering, because it's the reason a recap earns its place next to primary commentary.

If a well-made piece on the same story already exists, don't re-explain what it already explains
well — find what has happened **since** it was made, and make that the spine. Being a period newer
than the commentary, with your own data, is the edge; use it.
